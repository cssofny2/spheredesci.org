The funding infrastructure available to independent scientists is changing. In early 2026, the Sei Foundation launched the Sapien Open Science Fund I, committing $65 million to support science projects operating outside traditional academic grant structures. <Citation id="bex2026" /> The fund targets areas including user-owned research data, wearable instrumentation, and open-access methodologies — categories that overlap with the design space SPHERE is working in.

## What "alternative funding infrastructure" means in practice

Traditional grant cycles require institutional affiliation, established publication records, and proposals submitted against a fixed topic agenda. They work well for large research groups with existing overhead structures, but they can slow or block smaller independent experiments that do not fit neatly into a department's current priorities.

Alternative funding pools, including community science funds and foundation-backed programs like Sapien, evaluate projects differently. They often weight transparency of method, openness of data publication, and whether a project addresses a genuine gap in the existing literature — criteria that favor independent, pre-institutional work over credentialed institutional work.

For a project like SPHERE, which is at the proposal stage and not yet affiliated with a university lab, this shift in criteria matters. The proposed experiment is well-defined, the <a href="/protocol/">LOC-1 preregistration is on record at OSF</a>, and the measurement plan is publicly documented. That kind of upfront transparency is exactly what many alternative funders look for.

## Open science requirements as a feature, not a compliance burden

Several of the 2026 funding models explicitly require preregistration of hypotheses and blinded analysis plans as conditions of support. <Citation id="bex2026" /> These are not obstacles — they are the same practices that SPHERE's proposed protocol already incorporates.

The LOC-1 protocol requires that data collection and analysis decisions be locked before any measurement begins. An independent reviewer holds the unblinding key. Results, whether positive or null, must be reported as recorded. Funders who require this level of transparency are well-aligned with the kind of research SPHERE is designed to produce.

## What SPHERE's proposal stage means for funding eligibility

"Proposal stage" is sometimes read as "not yet ready." In the context of alternative funding, it can mean the opposite: the experiment is defined enough to evaluate, but no irreversible design decisions have been locked by prior investment. Funders can influence the protocol while it is still open.

That window is narrow. Once instrumentation is purchased and installation begins, the ability to negotiate changes to the measurement design narrows considerably. Projects like SPHERE that publish their full design in advance — the <a href="https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23583932">whitepaper is on Zenodo</a>, the protocol is on OSF — give potential funders and collaborators the information they need to engage before that window closes.

## Open questions for any independent experiment

Community funding infrastructure solves the access problem but does not solve the credibility problem on its own. A well-funded independent experiment is still subject to the same scrutiny as any other. The questions that matter remain: Is the proposed effect distinguishable from instrument noise? Is the uncertainty budget adequate? Are the controls sufficient to rule out the most plausible systematic explanations?

The SPHERE design addresses these questions in the <a href="/research/">research documentation</a> and the LOC-1 protocol, but those answers will require independent review before any result carries weight. Funding infrastructure makes the experiment possible; peer review is what makes the result credible.
