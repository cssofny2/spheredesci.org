The registered <a href="/protocol/">LOC-1 protocol</a> asks whether moving the same hollow copper sphere between fixed positions produces a repeatable resonance difference once measured conditions are controlled. A natural follow-up question is whether any such difference belongs to the place itself or to the environment the place delivers to the sphere: vibration, magnetic field, temperature and pressure. This note sets out a proposed way to answer that by recording at one location and replaying that environment at another.

## Two different signals

A passive copper sphere has no power source, so it does not emit a signal of its own. It has mechanical resonant modes, fixed by its geometry and elastic constants, and electromagnetic responses such as eddy currents and scattering. Anything measured at a location is the sphere's response to whatever excites it there. The experiment therefore records two separate things:

1. **The sphere's intrinsic signature**: resonance frequency f₀, quality factor Q and mode splitting. This is a property of the object.
2. **The site's environment as delivered to the sphere**: mount acceleration, magnetic field, temperature and pressure. This is a property of the place.

Replay tests whether the first depends on the second.

## Where the sphere resonates

The project's <a href="/resources/hollow-copper-sphere/">reference artifact</a> is a provisional 9.2 mm outer-diameter shell with a 0.3 mm wall. For a thin spherical shell, Lamb's analysis gives the breathing (ℓ = 0) mode: <Citation id="lamb1882" />

<Equation latex="f₀ = (1 / 2πR) · √[ 2E / (ρ(1 − ν)) ]" label="Thin-shell breathing mode" />

With E ≈ 128 GPa, ν ≈ 0.34 and ρ ≈ 8,960 kg/m³, the square-root term is about 6,580 m/s. For R = 4.6 mm that gives f₀ ≈ 228 kHz, consistent with the whitepaper's 230 kHz design band. Copper's modulus varies with alloy and temper (published values span roughly 117–130 GPa), which moves f₀ by about 5 percent. <Citation id="nistcu" /> The thin-shell spectrum also contains lower ℓ = 2 modes near 0.42 f₀ (about 95 kHz) and 0.70 f₀ (about 160 kHz), so a readout covering roughly 80–260 kHz is needed to identify modes correctly.

<DataPoint label="Estimated breathing mode, 9.2 mm shell" value="≈ 228" unit="kHz" note="Analytical free-shell estimate, roughly ±5–10%; mounting adds stiffness and damping. Must be confirmed by sweep or finite-element analysis." />

Every frequency scales as 1/R, which makes larger prototypes useful for debugging:

| Diameter | Shell f₀ | Solid ℓ = 2 | Solid f₀ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9.2 mm | ≈ 228 kHz | — | — |
| 5 cm | ≈ 42 kHz | ≈ 38 kHz | ≈ 82 kHz |
| 10 cm | ≈ 21 kHz | ≈ 19 kHz | ≈ 41 kHz |
| 30 cm | ≈ 7 kHz | ≈ 6.3 kHz | ≈ 14 kHz |

"Shell f₀" is the thin hollow shell breathing mode (the 9.2 mm row is the 0.3 mm-wall baseline); "Solid ℓ = 2" and "Solid f₀" are the quadrupole and breathing modes of a solid sphere. All values are illustrative free-sphere calculations, not measurements.

### Electromagnetic scale

For copper's resistivity, electromagnetic skin depth is about 8.4 mm at 60 Hz and about 0.14 mm at 230 kHz, so extremely-low-frequency (ELF) fields pass straight through a 0.3 mm wall. The lowest electromagnetic resonance of a 4.6 mm-radius sphere lies near c/(2πa), about 10 GHz, and its self-capacitance is about 0.5 pF. The fundamental Schumann resonance near 8 Hz has an amplitude that rarely exceeds about 1 pT. <Citation id="janssens2021" /> The sphere has no resonance in that band and no meaningful mechanism to store it.

## Recording at Location A

<ExperimentStep number="1" title="Thermal soak">
Install the sphere on its standard mount in a passive thermal enclosure and wait until artifact temperature drift falls below about 10 mK per hour. The project's <a href="/research/temperature-coefficient-copper-sphere-resonance/">temperature analysis</a> estimates roughly 15–38 Hz per kelvin at 230 kHz, so tens of millikelvin already matter.
</ExperimentStep>

<ExperimentStep number="2" title="Common timebase">
Lock every instrument to one reference, such as a GPS-disciplined oscillator or rubidium standard, and timestamp every channel in UTC.
</ExperimentStep>

<ExperimentStep number="3" title="Environment channels">
Record the location signature at the mount: a triaxial accelerometer on the mount (the control point to reproduce later) at 48 kHz or more and 24-bit depth; a geophone or seismometer on the floor for 0.5–50 Hz; a measurement microphone; an induction-coil magnetometer for 1–100 Hz plus a fluxgate for DC to about 1 kHz; a barometer, humidity sensor and two or three RTDs. Log at least 24 hours to capture diurnal cycles, with calibration factors, sensor serials, orientation and position stored as metadata.
</ExperimentStep>

<ExperimentStep number="4" title="Intrinsic resonance channel">
Measure the sphere by swept sine (small piezo or air-coupled ultrasonic exciter, read out by laser Doppler vibrometry or a contact pickup) and by ring-down (a tone burst at f₀ followed by an exponential-sinusoid fit for f₀ and Q). Commercial vibrometers cover the band comfortably. <Citation id="polytec" /> Repeat every 10–30 minutes through the ambient log so f₀ can be regressed against temperature, pressure and vibration.
</ExperimentStep>

<ExperimentStep number="5" title="Transfer path">
Tap-test or sweep the mount to obtain the site frequency-response function (FRF) from floor to sphere. The deliverables from Location A are a target time history and power spectral density (PSD) for every environment channel, f₀ and Q over time with their temperature regression, and the mount FRF.
</ExperimentStep>

## Replaying at Location B

<ExperimentStep number="6" title="Move, re-soak and record a native baseline">
Relocation is a thermal and mechanical event. Log the move, re-soak for the same duration as at A, then record B's ambient environment and f₀ exactly as at A. This is the B-native condition.
</ExperimentStep>

<ExperimentStep number="7" title="Isolate from Location B">
Place the sphere and mount on an isolation stage (air mounts or a negative-stiffness platform), inside an acoustic enclosure and a mu-metal shield or active three-axis field cancellation. Re-measure until B's residual ambient sits at least 20 dB below A's recorded level in every band to be replayed. With a 20 dB margin, an incoherent residual adds only about 0.04 dB to the replayed level; the worst coherent case is about 0.8 dB.
</ExperimentStep>

<ExperimentStep number="8" title="Mechanical replay by time waveform replication">
Drive the mount through an electrodynamic shaker. Measure the drive-to-control FRF H(f) with band-limited noise, compute an initial drive from a regularized inverse, then iterate the drive until the control accelerometer reproduces A's recorded waveform. This is the drive-file method used for road-load replay and standardized as Time Waveform Replication. <Citation id="milstd810h" />
</ExperimentStep>

<Equation latex="x(k+1) = x(k) + g · H⁻¹ · (y_target − y(k)),  with g ≈ 0.3–0.7 and H⁻¹ ≈ H* / (|H|² + λ)" label="Iterative inverse-FRF drive correction" />

<ExperimentStep number="9" title="Magnetic replay">
Replay A's recorded field with three-axis Helmholtz coils and close the loop with a magnetometer at the sphere position. For coils of radius 0.15 m with 50 turns, 1 mA produces about 0.3 µT, so a picotesla-level replay needs nanoamp-level current and a precision low-noise source.
</ExperimentStep>

<ExperimentStep number="10" title="Thermal match and measurement">
Hold the enclosure at A's mean temperature within about 20–50 mK, or correct f₀ with A's regression and propagate its uncertainty. Keep shaker and coil heat away from the sphere: drive heating is the most likely false positive. Then measure f₀ and Q with the same readout chain, settings and timebase used at A.
</ExperimentStep>

<WarningBox title="Overwhelm means fidelity, not power">
Driving the sphere far above A's recorded levels changes the experiment and can cause amplitude-dependent and thermal frequency shifts. The goal is for the sphere to see A's environment, not more energy than A had.
</WarningBox>

## Proposed verification criteria

| Metric | Proposed pass criterion |
|---|---|
| PSD match, each replayed channel | Within ±3 dB per one-third-octave band (±1 dB tight) |
| Time-domain fidelity | Normalized RMS error under 10 percent (under 20 percent for low-cost rigs) |
| Coherence, target vs measured | Above 0.9 across the replay band |
| Isolation margin | B residual at least 20 dB below replay level in each band |
| Thermal match | T_B − T_A within 50 mK, or corrected with propagated uncertainty |
| Outcome variable | Δf₀ against A, with a pre-registered smallest effect size of interest and an equivalence test |

These thresholds are engineering proposals, not values set by any standard cited here.

## Controls

- **Conditions:** B-native, B-isolated, B with A-replay, B with sham replay (amplifier on, drive muted or a phase-randomized surrogate with A's spectrum) and B with B-replay (B's own recording played back, to test the apparatus itself).
- **Blinding:** an independent operator assigns conditions from a sealed key and the analyst sees coded data, following the approach described in <a href="/research/blind-analysis-position-labels/">why LOC-1 hides the position labels</a>.
- **Order:** randomized balanced blocks, with A–B–A returns to check for hysteresis.
- **Reference artifact:** a matched sphere that never moves, so common-mode drift can be removed.
- **Pre-registration:** the effect-size threshold, equivalence margin and sham conditions would be fixed before any data collection.

## What the outcomes would mean

- **H₀, standard physics:** every Δf₀ is explained by temperature, mounting preload and pressure. Replaying micro-g vibration or picotesla-to-nanotesla fields produces no shift above the noise floor apart from heating.
- **H₁, environment-mediated:** B-native differs from A, and A-replay reduces the difference significantly more than sham does.
- **H₂, position-intrinsic:** a difference persists after complete environmental replay with every thermal and mounting control holding. Only this outcome would favour the locational hypothesis.

The locational hypothesis carries a low prior probability under standard physics, and no established mechanism allows an object to retain a place's frequency. The value of this design is that it turns a vague location effect into a falsifiable split between environmental and positional explanations, and a null result would be published with the same care as a positive one.

## Equipment tiers

Prices are approximate planning figures, not quotes.

**Low-cost prototype (about USD 2,000–4,000):** a 5–10 cm commercial hollow copper ball (f₀ roughly 20–40 kHz); a Red Pitaya-class signal generator and Bode analyzer; a 24-bit/192 kHz audio interface; piezo pickups and drivers; a MEMS or IEPE accelerometer; a geophone or small seismometer; a tactile transducer and class-D amplifier as the shaker; home-wound three-axis Helmholtz coils with a DAQ-driven current source; RTDs with a millikelvin-resolution logger, a barometer and a GPS-disciplined oscillator.

**Lab grade (roughly USD 60,000–200,000 or more):** a laser Doppler vibrometer; a vector network analyzer; triaxial IEPE accelerometers and a multichannel 24-bit DAQ; electrodynamic shakers with a controller supporting time waveform replication; an active isolation table; induction-coil and fluxgate magnetometers; a mu-metal shield or active field cancellation; a rubidium frequency standard and a millikelvin-controlled thermal enclosure.

## Analysis outline

The following Python sketch shows the processing chain: PSD estimation, an H1 FRF estimate with coherence, a regularized inverse, the replay iteration and a ring-down fit. It is illustrative and untested against hardware.

```python
import numpy as np
import scipy.signal as sig
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit

FS = 48_000  # environment channels; the 230 kHz readout needs a separate fast DAQ

def psd(x, nper=2**15):
    return sig.welch(x, FS, nperseg=nper)

def frf_h1(drive, resp, nper=2**14):
    f, pxx = sig.welch(drive, FS, nperseg=nper)
    _, pxy = sig.csd(drive, resp, FS, nperseg=nper)
    _, coh = sig.coherence(drive, resp, FS, nperseg=nper)
    return f, pxy / pxx, coh

def inv_filter(h, lam=1e-3):
    return np.conj(h) / (np.abs(h) ** 2 + lam * np.max(np.abs(h)) ** 2)

def twr(target, play_and_record, h_inv, g=0.5, iters=8):
    n = len(target)
    x = np.fft.irfft(h_inv * np.fft.rfft(target), n=n)
    for _ in range(iters):
        err = target - play_and_record(x)
        x = x + g * np.fft.irfft(h_inv * np.fft.rfft(err), n=n)
    return x

def ringdown_fit(t, v, f_guess):
    model = lambda t, a, tau, f, ph, c: a * np.exp(-t / tau) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * t + ph) + c
    p, cov = curve_fit(model, t, v, p0=[v.max(), 1e-3, f_guess, 0, 0])
    f0, tau = p[2], p[1]
    return f0, np.sqrt(cov[2, 2]), np.pi * f0 * tau  # f0, its standard error, Q
```

## Safety

Vibrometer lasers are typically Class 2; do not stare into the beam. Shaker amplifiers can deliver dangerous currents, so use fused outputs and interlocks. Watch for coil heating at amp-level currents, wear hearing protection for audio-band shakers, and keep replayed magnetic fields at environmental levels near anyone with an implanted medical device.
