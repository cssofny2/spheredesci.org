Every solid object has a natural pitch, like a bell. Tap a bell and it rings at one steady note. Physics calls that note the object's resonant frequency.

Standard physics says the note depends on what the object is made of, its shape, its temperature, and what surrounds it. It says the note does not depend on where the object sits.

Project Sphere asks a simple question. If you move an object and change nothing else, does its note change?

Sphere stands for Spatial Positioning Harmonic Empirical Resonance Experiment. It is an independent research project. Its plan is to answer that question in a specialized lab, with very sensitive measurement equipment, and to publish the answer, whatever it turns out to be.

Standard physics predicts that nothing changes. The project exists because nobody has tested the question carefully, and a careful test is possible.

The question is inspired by an idea in the Bashar material, presented by Darryl Anka. In that material, an object's location is described as part of the object's own vibrational makeup, not as something outside it. If that were true, the same object might ring at a very slightly different note in a different place.

We credit that source as the inspiration. We do not treat it as evidence, and mentioning it does not imply that Darryl Anka or anyone associated with that material endorses this project.

For us, it is a hypothesis. That means a claim that makes a prediction we can measure. The project studies passive copper objects. It does not study people, health effects, or any kind of teleportation.

The first experiment is called LOC-1. It is a proposed design, still waiting for independent review.

Two matching hollow copper spheres sit inside one shielded enclosure, held at a very steady temperature. One sphere never moves. It is the reference. The other is moved between two marked positions, forty centimeters apart.

A laser reads each sphere's note without touching it. That matters. Even a tiny sensor attached to a sphere would change its note. We compare the moved sphere to the reference sphere as a ratio, so that drift shared by both cancels out.

We plan to watch one clear resonance above two hundred thirty kilohertz. That is a proposed test band. It still has to be confirmed in calibration. One precise clock keeps every reading in step.

Small shifts are easy to create by accident. Temperature, how the sphere is mounted, radio noise, and clock drift can all fake a result. So the design is built to rule those out before anything is blamed on location.

The moves happen on a random schedule held by an independent person. The analyst sees only coded labels, so hopes and guesses cannot steer the result.

We add controls. Sham moves, where the sphere is lifted and put back in the same spot. Rotations. A dummy object. Deliberate temperature changes during calibration, to measure exactly how much heat moves the note. And logs of temperature, humidity, pressure, magnetic field and vibration.

We plan at least forty placements at each position.

Before any real data is collected, the analysis plan is written down and frozen on a public registry, the Open Science Framework. It names one test and one threshold in advance.

There is also a proposal, not yet part of the design, to measure four objects at the same moment with one shared clock. It is open for public review.

Before the test, we define what each outcome would mean.

If the two positions give the same note, within a tight and pre-agreed limit, that is a bounded null result. It says no location effect was found for this object, this resonance, this separation and this level of uncertainty. It does not prove the idea wrong in every form.

If a difference appears and survives every control, that is a candidate signal. A candidate signal is not a discovery. It triggers more checking, and an independent team must repeat the experiment before anyone makes a positive claim.

And if the equipment is too noisy to tell, or a control fails, the result is inconclusive, and we will say so. Bigger error bars will never be passed off as evidence of no effect.

Every deviation from the plan is logged publicly, with a date and a reason.

Measurements this sensitive take careful equipment and expert review. So the plan is to raise the money in three phases. Each phase opens only after the one before it is finished, and its scope and amount are published before it opens. Every figure here is a planning estimate, not a price quote.

Phase one is about eighty-six thousand dollars. It pays for independent review of the plan by outside experts and a statistician, building and qualifying the shielded, temperature-controlled setup with rented or partner-lab instruments, legal and accounting costs, the first blinded test, and publishing the results.

Phase two is four hundred fifty thousand to one point two million dollars. It is the next step. It opens and equips a dedicated lab, buys or rents more of the sensitive instruments, and hires the experts to run them.

Phase three is about two point one million dollars. That is the running total across all three phases, not a separate raise, and it is the top end of the budget. It covers the full lab, equipment and team needed to run the complete set of experiments and publish the results.

Why do this at all?

Because the answer matters either way.

If a real difference survives every check and is repeated by an independent team, it would mean that something about how objects relate to space is missing from today's physics. That would be a major discovery.

If no difference is found, that matters too. A clean, well-controlled no settles a question people keep debating. It tells other researchers where not to look, at this level of sensitivity. And it shows how to test an unusual idea fairly, by neither simply believing it nor simply dismissing it.

Either way, the project publishes the raw data, the environmental logs, the analysis code and a kit that lets anyone repeat the work. Results go on the website and an open preprint server within sixty days of unblinding, with the same prominence whatever they say.

Good science has to be able to report both answers.

No measurements have been taken yet, and there are no results. The project is at the proposal stage.

It needs independent protocol reviewers, people with precision-measurement and statistics expertise, partners who can offer instrument time, and people to help with documentation.
