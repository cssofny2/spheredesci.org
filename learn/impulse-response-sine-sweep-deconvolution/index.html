Impulse response measurement: sine sweeps and deconvolution

A recorded impulse response captures how an object responds, so it can be replayed, modelled or synthesised. It also shows the limits of what replay can do.

Added October 16, 2026 · Educational explainer

Status: educational explainer. This page reports no experiment results. Project S.P.H.E.R.E. is at the proposal stage, hypotheses are not findings, and nothing here amends the draft LOC-1 protocol.

What an impulse response is

If an object behaves as a linear, time-invariant system, its response to any input is fixed by one function: its impulse response, the output you would see after an ideal instantaneous tap. Its Fourier transform is the transfer function. Peaks in that transfer function are the resonances.

Why a sweep beats a click

A real tap is short, so it carries little energy and is limited by the hammer or speaker. A long sweep puts far more energy into each frequency while keeping the signal below the actuator's limits, which raises the signal-to-noise ratio. The exponential sine sweep method has a second advantage: harmonic distortion from a slightly nonlinear speaker or amplifier lands as separate lines before the linear response after deconvolution, so a time window can remove it. Angelo Farina described the method in detail for audio measurements.

x(t) = sin[ (w1*T / ln(w2/w1)) * ( exp((t/T) * ln(w2/w1)) - 1 ) ]

w1, w2: start and stop angular frequency    T: sweep duration

How the deconvolution works

  1. Play the sweep through the actuator and record the response with the sensor, using one shared clock.
  2. Build the inverse filter: the sweep reversed in time, with an amplitude envelope that offsets the sweep's pink spectrum.
  3. Convolve the recording with the inverse filter. The sweep compresses into an impulse, delayed by the sweep length.
  4. Window the result. The linear impulse response sits at the main peak; harmonic-distortion products arrive earlier and can be discarded or analysed separately.

Practical points: sweep beyond the band of interest at both ends, leave time after the sweep for the response to decay, keep levels below clipping, and measure at more than one level to check linearity.

What you can do with the impulse response

UseHowLimit
ConvolutionConvolve any input with the measured response to predict the outputPassive and linear. It reproduces the measured behaviour, no more.
Additive modal synthesisSum one damped sinusoid per modeNeeds accurate frequencies, damping and phases for every mode that matters.
Parametric fitFit frequencies and damping ratios, then compare repeatsMode count and model order must be chosen with care.
Inverse-filtered driveDesign an actuator signal that makes a sensor follow a target waveformNeeds regularisation; exact inversion of deep antiresonances is unstable.
Time reversalReverse a recorded response and re-emit it to refocus energy at a pointFocuses vibration; it does not retune the object.

The additive form is s(t) = Σ An e−t/τn sin(2π fn t + φn), where τn = 1/(2π ζn fn).

What none of this does: retune the object

Convolution and modal synthesis recreate the response that was measured. They do not alter the object that produced it. Changing a frequency in a synthesis model describes a different object, not a changed copy of the real one. Replaying a recorded response through an actuator drives the object at the frequencies in the recording, and the object rings down at its own natural frequencies when the drive stops. Time reversal on plates has been demonstrated to refocus a chirp-derived response at a chosen point, enough to make salt grains jump there, which is energy focusing rather than retuning.

Whether any treatment changed the object has to be settled by an independent low-amplitude measurement afterwards. See can sound change an object's resonant frequency?

In this project

The record-and-replay proposal uses these tools to test, not assume, whether replay changes anything. The full article covers the replay chain, the confounds and the proposed controls. At the project's proposed band above 230 kHz, a sweep-based measurement needs megasample-per-second acquisition and a shared reference clock. No measurement has been made.

Frequently asked questions

What is an exponential sine sweep?

A sine tone whose frequency rises exponentially with time, so each octave takes the same time. Recorded through a system and deconvolved, it yields the impulse response and separates harmonic distortion from the linear response.

Can an impulse response change an object's resonance?

No. An impulse response describes how the object behaves. Convolving with it, or synthesising from it, recreates that behaviour but does not alter the object that produced it.
