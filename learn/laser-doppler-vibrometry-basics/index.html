Laser Doppler vibrometer basics

A laser Doppler vibrometer measures how fast a surface moves by analyzing the shift in frequency of laser light reflected from it.

Added October 14, 2026 · Educational explainer

Status: educational explainer. This page reports no experiment results. Project S.P.H.E.R.E. is at the proposal stage, hypotheses are not findings, and nothing here amends the registered LOC-1 protocol.

How it works

A laser Doppler vibrometer (LDV) exploits the Doppler effect — the same principle that changes the pitch of a passing siren — to measure surface motion without physical contact. The instrument splits a laser beam into two paths: a measurement beam directed at the target surface and a reference beam retained inside the instrument. When the measurement beam reflects off the moving surface, its optical frequency shifts in proportion to the instantaneous velocity of that surface. Mixing the reflected beam with the reference beam produces an interference signal whose frequency encodes the surface velocity directly.

The decoder electronics — typically a frequency demodulator or a digital phase-locked loop — convert that interference signal into a voltage or digital output proportional to velocity. Integrating the velocity signal over time yields displacement. Differentiating yields acceleration. All three quantities are therefore available from a single optical measurement, and the dynamic range of modern instruments spans from sub-nanometer displacements to several meters per second — far beyond what most contact sensors can simultaneously cover.

A 3D scanning LDV (3D SLDV) adds two or three measurement heads, each viewing the target from a different angle. Trigonometric decomposition of their simultaneous signals reconstructs the full three-component velocity vector at each scan point. By automatically steering mirrors across a grid of points on the surface, the system builds a spatial map of vibration that reveals the full modal shape of the structure — which parts move in phase, which move out of phase, and with what amplitude at each resonant frequency.

Why non-contact measurement matters for resonance work

Every sensor attached to a vibrating structure modifies that structure. An accelerometer bolted to the wall of a thin copper sphere adds concentrated mass at that point. Mass lowers local resonant frequencies; a stiff mounting bracket raises them. Even a few grams on a small, lightweight shell can shift resonant frequencies by several hertz — more than enough to obscure the very effect being measured. This is called mass loading.

Optical measurement sidesteps mass loading entirely. The photons carrying the measurement beam carry negligible momentum compared with any vibrational force of interest. The sphere's dynamics remain unperturbed by the act of observation. That property is critical when the experiment aims to detect small differences between measurement conditions, because a contact sensor would introduce a systematic shift correlated with sensor placement that the experiment cannot easily separate from the signal of interest.

Non-contact measurement does introduce its own requirements. The surface must reflect enough laser light in the direction of the receiving optics. Copper's natural surface has moderate reflectivity in the visible and near-infrared, but oxidation or surface roughness can scatter the beam and reduce signal quality. Retroreflective tape, silver spray, or very fine polishing are common solutions, each with trade-offs for how they alter surface impedance. The vibrometer itself must be mechanically isolated from any vibration source in the environment, because its own motion is indistinguishable from target motion at the detector. Optical bench isolation, vibration-absorbing feet, or locating the instrument on a separate structure are standard mitigations.

Interferometry and demodulation in detail

The optical core of a Michelson-type LDV is a laser interferometer. Coherent laser light from a single-mode diode or HeNe source is split by a beamsplitter. One arm carries the measurement beam to the target; the other forms the reference path within the instrument. Coherence length of the laser must exceed the optical path-length difference between the two arms, which is why LDV sources are always single-frequency, high-coherence lasers rather than broadband sources.

The returning measurement beam and the reference beam are recombined at the beamsplitter and focused onto a photodetector. If the surface is stationary, the interference fringe is static. If the surface moves toward the instrument, the reflected beam is blue-shifted and the fringe pattern cycles at the Doppler frequency fD = 2v/λ, where v is the surface velocity and λ is the laser wavelength. At a typical HeNe wavelength of 632.8 nm, a surface velocity of 1 mm/s produces a Doppler frequency of about 3.16 kHz — well within the audio band and easy to demodulate electronically.

Because the Doppler shift is proportional to velocity component along the beam axis, the measurement captures only the component of motion along the line of sight. Tilting the beam relative to the surface motion reduces the apparent velocity by the cosine of the angle. For a 3D SLDV this geometric factor is handled by the vector decomposition across multiple heads, but for a single-axis instrument it must be accounted for in calibration and data reduction.

Common error sources and mitigations

Understanding where LDV measurements go wrong is as important as understanding how they work, because uncontrolled errors look identical to real signals in the data.

  • Speckle dropout. Laser light scattered from a rough surface creates a speckle pattern. As the surface moves laterally (transverse to the beam), the speckle pattern shifts and can temporarily reduce signal intensity to near zero — a speckle dropout. Modern vibrometers include signal-quality monitors and data-quality flags that mark dropout events. Good retroreflective preparation of the surface reduces their frequency.
  • Beam angle error. If the measurement beam is not perpendicular to the intended surface, the velocity reading is reduced by the cosine of the angle. For scanning measurements on a curved surface like a sphere, the angle between beam and surface normal varies across the scan grid. Most analysis software corrects for this if the geometry is properly defined, but errors in the geometry model propagate directly into the velocity map.
  • Instrument vibration. The vibrometer housing itself must be rigid and mounted on an isolated surface. Any vibration of the instrument body appears as spurious target motion. A common test is to point the instrument at a mirror known to be stationary and verify that the background noise level meets specification.
  • Environmental acoustics. Sound waves in the air between the instrument and the target cause air-column refractive-index fluctuations that add phase noise to the measurement beam. This is generally small at audio frequencies but can be significant at higher frequencies or in noisy environments. A partial acoustic enclosure or a nitrogen-purged optical path reduces it.
  • Thermal drift. The laser wavelength drifts slightly with temperature. For velocity measurements the fractional drift is the same as the fractional wavelength shift — typically parts per million per degree Celsius for stabilized lasers — which is negligible for vibration work. For static displacement measured by fringe counting it matters more.

3D SLDV and modal analysis

Modal analysis is the process of identifying a structure's natural frequencies, damping ratios, and mode shapes from vibration data. The mode shape — the spatial pattern of relative displacement at each resonant frequency — is what distinguishes, for example, the lowest breathing mode of a sphere from a bending mode or a torsional mode. Contact accelerometer arrays can provide mode shapes too, but they require many individual sensors and their mass loading shifts both frequency and shape.

A 3D SLDV replaces the accelerometer array with a single set of optical heads that scan hundreds or thousands of points automatically. The software correlates the velocity response at each point with a reference signal (either a force sensor signal or a fixed reference point on the structure) and extracts the complex frequency-response function. Curve-fitting algorithms then decompose that function into contributions from individual modes, yielding frequency, damping, and shape for each mode in the measurement band.

For a hollow copper sphere the expected mode families include radial (breathing) modes, spheroidal modes, and torsional modes. Finite-element models predict these frequencies based on shell geometry, wall thickness, material properties (density, Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio), and boundary conditions. The 3D SLDV measurement checks whether the actual frequencies and shapes match the model — a process called model validation — and reveals any manufacturing asymmetries that break the theoretical degeneracy of modes that are identical in a perfect sphere.

In this project

Project S.P.H.E.R.E. proposes using 3D SLDV as the primary vibrometric measurement instrument for resonance characterization of hollow copper spheres. The registered LOC-1 protocol specifies this instrument class as part of the measurement plan. No instrument has been purchased or installed on the basis of these pages; instrument selection remains a proposal. See the hardware guide and the equipment plan for the current proposal details.
