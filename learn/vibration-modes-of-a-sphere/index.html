Vibration modes of a sphere: copper sphere resonance basics

A solid elastic sphere has a whole family of vibration modes. Their frequencies follow from its radius, density and elastic properties.

Added October 14, 2026 · Educational explainer

Status: educational explainer. This page reports no experiment results. Project S.P.H.E.R.E. is at the proposal stage, hypotheses are not findings, and nothing here amends the draft LOC-1 protocol.

A family of modes

An elastic sphere can vibrate in torsional modes (twisting) and spheroidal modes (including the radial "breathing" mode, where the surface moves in and out). Horace Lamb analyzed these in the nineteenth century. Each mode has a characteristic pattern and frequency.

What sets the frequencies

For a given mode, frequency scales roughly with the speed of sound in the material divided by the sphere's radius. A larger sphere rings lower; a stiffer or lighter material rings higher. Machining tolerances, internal stress and mounting shift the real values from ideal ones, which is why each physical sphere needs its own calibration.

Why copper?

Copper is easy to obtain in high purity, machines well and has well-characterized elastic properties. Its frequencies change with temperature, so temperature recording and control are essential. These are practical reasons for a test object, not claims about copper being special.

Practical measurement issues

  • Suspend or support the sphere so the mount does not dominate the response.
  • Identify which mode is being measured and track neighboring peaks.
  • Record temperature, humidity and handling history.
