Preregistration explained: why plan before you measure

Preregistration means writing down your hypothesis, design and analysis plan, and time-stamping it, before you look at the outcome data.

Added October 14, 2026 · Educational explainer

Status: educational explainer. This page reports no experiment results. Project S.P.H.E.R.E. is at the proposal stage, hypotheses are not findings, and nothing here amends the draft LOC-1 protocol.

What a preregistration contains

A typical preregistration states the question, the hypotheses, how many measurements will be taken, what counts as an outlier, which statistical test will be used and what result would count as support or no support. It is filed with a timestamp so later readers can see what was decided in advance.

Why it matters

When an analysis is chosen after seeing the data, it is easy, even without intending to, to pick the version that looks most interesting. This inflates false positives. A preregistered plan separates confirmatory tests (planned) from exploratory ones (found along the way), and both are legitimate if labeled honestly.

Registered reports

A registered report goes one step further: a journal reviews the question and methods first and agrees in principle to publish regardless of the outcome. This reduces pressure to produce a positive result.

Preregistration is not a guarantee

A preregistration can be vague, can be broken, or can be filed after the fact. Readers should check whether deviations are reported. Preregistration improves transparency; it does not by itself make a design sound.

How S.P.H.E.R.E. approaches it

The draft LOC-1 protocol is written to be preregistered before any data collection. No preregistration has been filed yet, and none should be assumed to exist until a public record is linked. See also blinding in experiments.
