Q factor (quality factor) of a resonator, explained

Q factor tells you how sharp a resonance is and how long it rings. Here is the definition, the formulas that connect Q to bandwidth, damping and decay time, and how Q is measured.

Added November 4, 2026 · Educational explainer

Status: educational explainer. This page reports no experiment results. Project S.P.H.E.R.E. is at the proposal stage, hypotheses are not findings, and nothing here amends the registered LOC-1 protocol.

Q factor in one paragraph

Q factor (quality factor) measures how sharply a resonator responds near its natural frequency and how slowly it loses energy. A high-Q resonator has a narrow resonance peak and rings for many cycles after it is struck. A low-Q resonator has a broad peak and its ringing dies away quickly. Q is dimensionless, so a guitar string, a quartz crystal and a copper sphere can be compared on the same scale.

Definition and key formulas

Q has two equivalent definitions, one in the frequency domain and one in terms of energy. For a lightly damped resonator they agree:

Q = f0 / Δf                     f0: resonant frequency, Δf: full width at half power (-3 dB)
Q = 2π × (energy stored) / (energy lost per cycle)
ζ = 1 / (2Q)                       damping ratio
τ_amp = Q / (π f0)                  amplitude decay time constant, A(t) = A0 exp(-t/τ_amp)
τ_energy = Q / (2π f0)              energy decays twice as fast as amplitude

Two consequences are worth remembering. First, Q is roughly the number of radians of oscillation over which the stored energy falls by a factor of e, so after about Q/π cycles the amplitude has dropped to 1/e of its starting value. Second, the same Q appears in both domains: a narrow peak and a long ring-down are two views of one property.

A worked example (illustrative arithmetic)

Suppose a mode had f0 = 10 kHz and Q = 1,000. These numbers are chosen to show the arithmetic and are not a measurement of any real object.

QuantityFormulaResult
Half-power bandwidthΔf = f0 / Q10 Hz
Damping ratioζ = 1 / (2Q)0.0005
Amplitude time constantτ = Q / (π f0)about 31.8 ms
Energy time constantQ / (2π f0)about 15.9 ms
Cycles to reach 1/e amplitudeQ / πabout 318 cycles

Halve the Q and the peak doubles in width while the ring-down time halves. Nothing about the resonant frequency itself has to change.

Typical Q values

SystemApproximate Q
Critically damped door closerabout 0.5
Tuning forkaround 1,000
Atomic clocks and superconducting RF cavitiesup to 1011 and beyond

These are order-of-magnitude figures from general references. The Q of a particular object depends on its material, mounting, surroundings and the mode measured, so a real value always has to be measured, not looked up.

Why Q matters for precision frequency measurement

A resonance is only useful as a measuring instrument if its centre can be found precisely. A narrower peak gives the fitting routine a sharper feature to lock onto, so, other things equal, a higher Q makes a small frequency shift easier to resolve. In practice the achievable precision also depends on signal-to-noise ratio, drive stability, temperature drift and how long the signal is recorded. Q is one input to the measurement uncertainty, not a guarantee of low uncertainty.

Q also works as a health check. A resonance whose Q suddenly drops after a mount is re-seated, or that varies with drive amplitude, is telling you something changed in the losses, which may matter more than any small shift in frequency. Reporting Q alongside frequency makes a result easier to reproduce and to compare across independent replications.

What lowers Q in a real resonator

  • Internal friction in the material, including thermoelastic damping.
  • Support and mounting losses, where vibration leaks into a stand, string, pad or clamp.
  • Fluid loading: viscous and radiation losses to the surrounding air or gas.
  • Sensor and actuator loading: a bonded sensor or a coupled drive adds mass and loss.
  • Surface and joint losses from coatings, contamination and imperfect bonds.

Because mounting often dominates, the same sphere on two different supports can show quite different Q values. That is one reason a careful support design matters before any comparison between conditions.

How to measure Q

  1. Frequency sweep, half-power width. Drive the resonator across the peak, record the response, and fit a resonance curve. Q is f0 divided by the fitted full width at half power. Sweep slowly enough that the response settles at each frequency, otherwise the peak is distorted and Q is underestimated.
  2. Ring-down. Excite the mode, remove the drive, and record the decaying signal. Fit an exponential to the envelope to get τ, then Q = π f0 τ. This works best when one mode dominates and the record lasts several time constants.
  3. Impulse response. Measure the impulse response with a tap or an exponential sine sweep and deconvolution, then fit damped sinusoids to extract frequency and damping for each mode.

Whichever method is used, check these points:

  • Amplitude independence. Repeat at two or more drive levels. If Q changes with amplitude, the system is not behaving linearly and a single Q is not meaningful.
  • Frequency resolution. An FFT of a record of length T resolves about 1/T in frequency. To resolve a 10 Hz wide peak you need a record comfortably longer than 0.1 s.
  • Closely spaced modes. A perfect sphere has degenerate modes, and small imperfections split them. Two unresolved neighbouring peaks look like one broad, low-Q peak. See vibration modes of a sphere.
  • Temperature. Drift during a slow sweep moves the peak while it is being measured and broadens it artificially.
  • Loaded versus unloaded Q. The Q seen through a sensor and drive includes their losses. Say which one is reported.

Q in spherical resonators

Spherical resonators have a long history in precision metrology. Gas-filled spherical acoustic resonators, for example, have been used at national standards laboratories to determine the speed of sound in gases, and the work analysed both the resonance frequencies and their widths, which carry the loss information. Q is therefore a standard quantity to report for a sphere, and the half-width of each mode has to be understood to interpret the frequency. The NIST high-frequency acoustic resonator article on this site discusses Q and coupling for a very different device class.

In this project

Project S.P.H.E.R.E. proposes to compare the measured resonance of a controlled copper sphere at different positions. Q is relevant to that design as a characterisation quantity and as a confound check: if the losses changed between conditions, for instance because the support was re-seated, the width of a resonance could shift even if the frequency did not. No Q value has been measured for the project's sphere. Frequently asked questions

What is Q factor?

Q factor, or quality factor, is a dimensionless number describing how weakly damped a resonator is. It equals the resonant frequency divided by the width of the resonance peak at half power, and it is roughly 2π times the energy stored divided by the energy lost per cycle. A higher Q means a narrower peak and a longer ring-down.

What is a good Q factor?

It depends on the application. A higher Q gives a sharper peak that makes small frequency shifts easier to see, but no single value is good for everything. A tuning fork is often quoted near 1,000, while atomic clocks and superconducting cavities reach far higher values. Always state the mode, frequency and conditions when quoting a Q.

How do you convert Q factor to damping ratio?

For a lightly damped resonator the damping ratio is ζ = 1/(2Q). A Q of 500 corresponds to ζ = 0.001. Resonators with Q below 1/2 are overdamped and do not oscillate.

How is Q factor measured?

The three common routes are fitting the half-power width of a frequency-response peak, fitting the exponential decay of a ring-down, and fitting a measured impulse response. Each should be repeated at several drive levels to check that Q does not depend on amplitude.

Does Q factor change the resonant frequency?

Slightly. The damped frequency is f0·√(1 − 1/(4Q²)), which is lower than the undamped frequency by a fraction of about 1/(8Q²). For Q = 1,000 that is roughly 1.25 parts in ten million, usually far smaller than other uncertainties.
