Hollow copper sphere: why, how it is made, purity, size and cost

The project’s reference artifact is a thin-walled, sealed copper shell. This page sets out why, what purity it needs, how it could be made, how big it is and what it may cost.

Added October 16, 2026 · Proposal stage

Status: proposal-stage design baseline. This page reports no measurements and no supplier quotations, and it does not amend the draft LOC-1 protocol. Dimensions are design estimates, and the cost figure is an unquoted planning range. Hypotheses are not findings.

Why a hollow copper sphere?

The experiment compares the resonant frequency of a test sphere at two positions against a matched, stationary reference sphere. The artifact therefore has to be simple to model, easy to match and quiet. A hollow copper shell fits that job for five reasons.

  • Known geometry. A sphere has no preferred orientation, and its vibration modes have been analyzed in closed form since Horace Lamb (1881). See vibration modes of a sphere.
  • Reachable frequency. A thin-shell breathing mode near 230 kHz needs a radius of about 4.6 mm, a size that is practical to make, mount and scan.
  • Low mass, fast thermal settling. The baseline shell weighs about 0.67 g, while a solid copper sphere of the same size would weigh about 3.6 g. Less mass settles thermally sooner, and temperature is a first-order error source in the error budget.
  • Well-characterized material. Copper is available in high purity, has well-documented elastic and thermal properties, and can be welded under vacuum.
  • A ratio measurand. Matched shells let the analysis use r = fT/fR, which cancels drift common to both spheres. Matching reduces common-mode sensitivity but does not make the spheres identical.

Trade-offs. A thin wall is fragile and easy to distort. The weld seam is a known perturbation that must be recorded and tracked. Copper’s elastic modulus changes with temperature. These are practical reasons to choose copper, not claims that it is special.

Dimensions, size, weight and density

The whitepaper’s thin-shell approximation is fbreathing ≈ (1/2πR)·√(2E/ρ(1−ν)). With E = 130 GPa, ρ = 8,960 kg/m³ and ν = 0.34, a 230 kHz breathing mode implies R ≈ 4.6 mm. The table applies that formula to the baseline and to larger shells with an illustrative wall of about 3% of the diameter. Masses use 8.94 g/cm³.

Larger shells have a breathing mode far below 230 kHz. They would have to use higher-order modes, which the whitepaper allows, but those need a mode survey rather than a one-line formula.

What moves these numbers

  • Elastic modulus. Handbook values for annealed oxygen-free copper are near 117 GPa, not 130 GPa. That alone lowers the baseline estimate by about 5%, to roughly 218 kHz. The modulus of each lot must be measured.
  • Wall thickness. Shell mass scales with wall thickness, so a 0.02 mm wall difference changes mass by about 7%. Weighing each half before welding is a cheap matching check.
  • Density. C10100 is specified at 8.94 g/cm³ and pure copper is 8.96 g/cm³. The 0.2% difference is negligible next to wall tolerance.
  • Thin-shell validity. The formula assumes a thin wall (t/R of about 0.065 here). Finite-element modelling must confirm the mode and its neighbors.

C10200 OFAt least 99.95% Cu, oxygen at most 10 ppmNot preferred. Use only with a documented trade-off.

Impurities at the parts-per-million level change copper’s modulus very little. The reason to specify high purity is lot-to-lot reproducibility, low oxygen and few inclusions, which help four spheres behave alike. It is not a claim that purity affects the hypothesis.

A purity label is not a specification. The whitepaper requires procurement to document chemical purity, oxygen content, fabrication process, wall thickness, surface treatment and residual stress separately. Grain size and anneal condition affect modal frequency and damping as much as the chemistry does. All four spheres should come from one heat or lot.

How it would be manufactured

No fabricator has been engaged. The sequence below is a proposed baseline for review.

  1. Certified stock. Lot-traced copper with mill certificate and independent analysis.
  2. Form the halves. At 9 mm, machining from bar or deep drawing is more realistic than spinning, which suits larger shells.
  3. Machine weld lips and record each half’s wall map and mass.
  4. Clean and etch. Degrease, acid etch, deionized rinse and dry.
  5. Vacuum electron-beam weld. Weld at the equator. This also seals the cavity at low pressure.
  6. Vacuum anneal. Relieve stress and record the time and temperature.
  7. Finish. A satin, low-gloss surface gives the vibrometer a usable return.
  8. Inspect and document. Mass, dimensions, wall map, weld inspection, leak check and a vibrometer mode survey.

What it would cost to manufacture

Frequently asked questions

Why is the S.P.H.E.R.E. test artifact hollow rather than solid?

A thin shell has a small mass and a short thermal time constant, and its breathing mode is easy to excite and read out. A solid sphere of the same outer size would be far heavier and would settle thermally much more slowly. The trade-off is a fragile wall and a weld seam that must be tracked.

What copper purity does the sphere need?

The whitepaper proposes 99.999% (5N) copper. C10100 oxygen-free electronic copper (at least 99.99% Cu, oxygen at most 5 ppm) is the widely stocked grade and the likely practical fallback. Either way, purity alone is not enough: oxygen content, process, wall thickness, surface treatment and residual stress must be documented separately.

How big and heavy is the sphere?

The provisional baseline is a 9.2 mm outer diameter with a 0.3 mm wall, which has a mass of about 0.67 g at 8.94 g/cm³ and a thin-shell breathing mode near 229 kHz. These are design estimates. Finite-element modelling and measured mode shapes will set the final geometry.

How much will it cost to make?

The copper itself costs well under a dollar. Cost comes from forming, welding, annealing and inspection, plus scrap. An unquoted planning range for a set of four is USD 10,000 to 40,000. It is not a quote or a budget line, and written supplier quotations are required first.
