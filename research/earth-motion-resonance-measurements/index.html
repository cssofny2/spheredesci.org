Does Earth’s motion affect resonance measurements?

Earth moves, but movement through space does not by itself establish that a copper sphere’s local resonance should change. The useful experimental question is whether a specified differential signal survives known sources of variation.

Added October 9, 2026 · Proposal stage · Draft for public review

Status: proposal for public review. This page contains no experiment results, community responses, installed-hardware claims or published findings. Hypotheses are not findings, and nothing here amends the current draft LOC-1 protocol.

Three motions, three different questions

Earth’s rotation changes a laboratory’s orientation relative to distant stars. Its orbit changes the laboratory’s motion and orientation relative to the Sun. The solar system’s galactic motion adds another reference frame. These descriptions do not identify an absolute resting place or, on their own, predict a measurable copper-sphere frequency shift.

In a local, co-moving apparatus, ordinary uniform translational motion is not an intrinsic resonance-setting parameter under Lorentz-invariant physics. Rotation, acceleration, gravity, thermal gradients, mounting stress and relative motion within the measurement chain require separate treatment. Precision relativity experiments do investigate orientation-dependent frequency ratios; their results must not be repackaged as proof of this project’s unverified location hypothesis.

Source: Humboldt University: Optical test of Lorentz invariance

What simultaneous acquisition solves—and what it does not

If objects are measured one after another, changes between acquisitions can be mistaken for differences between positions. Parallel acquisition lets us compare objects over the same observation window. A shared disturbance can then partly cancel in a ratio or differential measurement. Cancellation depends on the objects and channels responding similarly; four timestamps alone do not guarantee it.

Source: NI: Sample Clock Synchronization

Simultaneous acquisition does not freeze Earth, remove a temperature gradient, equalize two mounts, or eliminate orientation-dependent effects. It also suppresses some common signals: an effect that shifts every object equally may disappear from a frequency ratio. The protocol must state whether it tests differential position effects or common temporal effects.

Write a model before collecting data

A useful starting model is fᵢ(t) = fᵢ,₀ + L(positionᵢ, orientationᵢ, t) + βᵢ ΔTᵢ(t) + bᵢ(t) + εᵢ(t). Here L is the proposed effect, βᵢ is a calibrated temperature sensitivity, bᵢ includes instrumental and mounting biases, and εᵢ is residual noise. This is a proposed bookkeeping model, not a validated description or fitted project result.

A claim about galactic position needs a named coordinate system, an explicit dependence of L on that system, a predicted effect size and a measurable timescale. “Earth moved between readings” is not sufficiently specific to distinguish a hypothesis from clock drift.

Relativity is real, but scale matters

NIST has compared optical clocks at small changes of height and relative speed. A 2023 optical-clock study made synchronous comparisons across five ensembles spanning 1 cm and still evaluated spatial systematics. These are highly sensitive atomic-clock measurements, not copper-shell mechanical-resonance measurements. No transfer of their sensitivity to this proposed apparatus is justified.

Source: NIST: Relativity and Optical ClocksSource: A lab-based test of the gravitational redshift with a miniature clock network (2023)

Questions for public review

  • Which reference frame should the alternative hypothesis use, and what change should it predict?
  • Have you mistaken a time-dependent drift for a difference between locations? What revealed the error?
  • Would you first test a differential effect, or retain a separate absolute-frequency diagnostic? Explain the tradeoff.
