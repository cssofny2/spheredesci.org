What a miniature clock network teaches SPHERE about synchronized measurements

A published clock-network experiment supports the measurement strategy of synchronous differential comparison—not the existence of a copper-sphere locational effect.

Added October 9, 2026 · Proposal stage · Draft for public review

Status: proposal for public review. This page contains no experiment results, community responses, installed-hardware claims or published findings. Hypotheses are not findings, and nothing here amends the current draft LOC-1 protocol.

The article we are responding to

“A lab-based test of the gravitational redshift with a miniature clock network,” published in Nature Communications in 2023, compared five strontium atomic ensembles spaced 2.5 mm apart over a total vertical span of 1 cm. The researchers collected ten pairwise clock comparisons synchronously and used a blinded analysis.

Source: A lab-based test of the gravitational redshift with a miniature clock network (2023)

What the researchers found

The measured fractional frequency gradient was consistent with the gravitational redshift predicted by general relativity. The experiment evaluated magnetic-field, thermal-radiation, atomic-density and optical-lattice effects. The key point for our design is that synchronized measurements and a shared environment reduced some noise while spatial systematics still required measurement and correction.

Source: A lab-based test of the gravitational redshift with a miniature clock network (2023)

Our original response: four design lessons

  1. Make concurrency a verified capability. Record aligned observation windows and validate timing across every readout path.
  2. Measure gradients instead of assuming the enclosure is uniform. A common box does not mean identical conditions at every object.
  3. Account for correlated comparisons. More pairs are useful diagnostics but are not automatically independent evidence.
  4. Finalize analysis and uncertainty before unblinding. Otherwise an expected signal can influence correction choices.

These are proposed translations of methodological lessons to SPHERE. They are not quotations, a claim of collaboration, or proof that the proposed hardware achieves optical-clock performance.

Where the analogy breaks

Atomic transition frequencies are not elastic shell modes. Their interrogation techniques, signal quality and systematic shifts differ. SPHERE must establish its own temperature coefficient, mode stability, excitation repeatability and estimator performance. The published clock experiment tested a quantitative established prediction; the proposed position hypothesis still needs a comparable quantitative prediction.

A better public discussion

  • Have you used a reference sensor to cancel common noise? Did the sensors respond equally?
  • Would two references reveal problems a single reference misses? Explain your reasoning.
  • What result would change your opinion: a reproducible residual, an uncertainty bound, or a successful independent replication?

No community responses have been collected or published yet. These are invitations for review, not testimonials or a poll result.
